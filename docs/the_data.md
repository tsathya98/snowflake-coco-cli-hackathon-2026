# The data: what it represents, how it was made, and what was planted

Written for a reviewer who does not work in supply chain. The mechanism this project argues for is
domain-agnostic, but the demonstration is not, and an argument you cannot follow is not an argument.

Everything here is generated by [`sql/10_synthetic_data.sql`](../sql/10_synthetic_data.sql) at
provisioning time. **No real, proprietary, customer or personal data is used anywhere**, and no
Snowflake Marketplace listing is used either — see [`data_licences.md`](data_licences.md) for why
that is deliberate rather than an omission.

## The situation being modelled

A manufacturer buys components from six suppliers, holds stock of six finished goods, and — because
it is a regulated industry — sometimes quarantines a batch pending investigation.

Three things can go wrong, and somebody has to notice each of them:

- **A supplier starts delivering late.** Not one shipment, a *trend*. Nobody notices on the day; it
  shows up as a rolling average sliding.
- **Stock is heading for zero.** The question is never "is it zero" but "will it be, before the
  replacement lands".
- **A quarantined batch has been sitting there.** Someone has to chase it. Nobody owns "check
  whether anything has been forgotten".

All three are *exception* work: routine to handle, expensive to miss, and each requires reading
several tables plus a written procedure to decide what to do. That is precisely the shape of work an
agent should take on — and precisely the shape nobody dares automate, because the third one touches
records a regulator cares about.

## Glossary

The five terms that carry the demo.

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **On-time rate** | Share of a supplier's deliveries that arrived by the promised date. The baseline here is ~92%. |
| **Safety stock** | The floor a business wants to keep. Going below it is not an outage — it is the point at which one late delivery *becomes* an outage. |
| **Days of cover** | How many days current stock lasts at the current consumption rate. The number an operations team actually watches, because 41,000 units means nothing without knowing you burn 8,200 a day. |
| **Quality hold** | A batch quarantined pending investigation. It cannot ship until someone qualified dispositions it. Its **lot reference** identifies the physical batch. |
| **Robust *z*-score** | How much of an outlier a value is, measured with median and MAD rather than mean and standard deviation — because the outlier you are hunting inflates the very spread you would be measuring it against. |

Identifiers read as you would expect: `SUP-002` a supplier, `SKU-1003` a product, `QH-0034` a
quality hold, `RB-002` an operating procedure.

## Why *this* domain

Because it is the cleanest available case of one workload spanning three governance levels at once:

| Table | Tag | Why that tag |
|---|---|---|
| `SHIPMENTS`, `SUPPLIERS`, `SKUS`, `OPS_REQUESTS` | `open` | Commercial data. Chasing a late delivery is routine and reversible. |
| `INVENTORY` | `internal` | Raising a replenishment commits spend and a delivery slot. Recoverable, but somebody should decide. |
| `QUALITY_HOLDS` | `regulated` | A hold's disposition is exactly the kind of record a regulator audits. No automated system alters one. |
| `RUNBOOKS` | *untagged* | **Deliberately left classified by nobody**, to exercise the path where untagged is treated as demanding approval rather than as cleared. |

A single-classification dataset could not demonstrate the point. The agent has to span all of them
in one pass, and be seen to behave differently in each, from the same code.

## How it is generated

Two choices in [`sql/10_synthetic_data.sql`](../sql/10_synthetic_data.sql) are worth knowing.

**Pseudo-randomness comes from `HASH()`, not `RANDOM()`.** Snowflake's `RANDOM()` requires a
constant seed, so `RANDOM(<column>)` is a compilation error. `HASH()` accepts columns and is
deterministic — which means **provisioning twice produces byte-identical data, and the planted
anomalies always land in the same place.** A judge reproducing this gets the numbers in this
repository, not numbers like them.

**Volumes:** 2,400 shipments across 180 days, 6 suppliers, 6 SKUs, 40 quality holds, 5 operating
procedures. Small enough to reason about; large enough that a rolling baseline means something.

## What is planted, and why saying so matters

**We planted the anomalies the agent then detects.** That is worth stating plainly, because it
sounds like cheating until you look at what the alternative would be.

Two are planted:

| Planted | How |
|---|---|
| **SUP-002 degrades** | On-time collapses to ~35% for the last 13 days. Every other supplier stays near the 92% baseline. |
| **SKU-1003 runs down** | 41,000 units on hand against a safety stock of 90,000, consuming 8,200 a day — 5.0 days of cover. |

The aging quality holds are not planted so much as *dated*: the generator spreads hold ages across a
range, and four of the forty land past the sixty-day threshold RB-003 sets.

A testbed with no known-bad data cannot demonstrate detection at all, and one seeded from a real
operation would fail the clean-room rule this project is built under. So the question is not whether
the anomalies were placed — they were — but whether **the detector had to do real work to find
them.** Three things say it did:

1. **The threshold was not chosen to fit.** Every detector threshold is quoted from a clause in the
   operating-procedure corpus: RB-001's twenty percentage points, RB-002's fourteen days of cover,
   RB-003's sixty days. The documents were written before the data, and a threshold picked to make a
   demo work is the exact failure that rule exists to prevent.
2. **The separation is real, and measured.** SUP-002 scores a robust *z* of **−3.63**. The
   next-worst supplier scores **−0.46**. That is not a flag being read back; it is an outlier
   against a population that also contains ordinary variation.
3. **The interesting cases are the ones with no anomaly.** `eval/cases.json` includes a stockout
   where 90,000 units are *already in transit* — where the correct answer is to do nothing, because
   RB-002 calls duplicate replenishment the most common error in the workflow. A detector tuned to
   find what was planted would fail that one. It passes.

And the routing — which is what this project is actually about — does not depend on the anomalies at
all. It depends on the tags. Re-tag `SHIPMENTS` as `regulated` and the same detected exception
routes somewhere different, with nothing in the data changed.

## What a real deployment would need

Stated because a reviewer will ask, and finding it stated is worth more than finding it hidden.

- The corpus is five procedures **written for this project**. A real deployment would need the
  organisation's live controlled documents, under the same document control — which changes nothing
  architecturally, and everything about who signs off.
- Volumes are demonstrative. The detection is set-based — one `MERGE` per detector — so cost tracks
  data volume rather than exception count, but that is an argument about the shape of the SQL rather
  than a measurement at scale.
- One account, one region. Nothing here is multi-tenant.
